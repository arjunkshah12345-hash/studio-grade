Nine skies. One switch.

In 2001 the amateur astronomer John E. Bortle published a nine-class scale for judging a night sky by what you can actually see in it.1 Class 9 is a city centre. Class 1 is as dark as Earth gets. Keep scrolling to walk out of town, and the sky behind this page follows the scale.

  1. 9

    Class 9: Inner-city sky

    Faintest star · magnitude 4.0 at best

    The whole sky glows. Beyond the Moon, the planets and the brightest stars, there is little to find, and most constellations are missing pieces. The Little Dipper is down to Polaris and the two stars at the end of its bowl.

  2. 8

    Class 8: City sky

    Faintest star · magnitude 4.1 to 4.5

    The sky is whitish grey or orange, bright enough to read a headline by. Familiar patterns are there but thin, and the faint constellations are gone.

  3. 7

    Class 7: Suburban/urban transition

    Faintest star · magnitude 4.6 to 5.0

    A grey-white glow covers everything, with light domes in every direction. The Milky Way is gone, or nearly. The Andromeda Galaxy can be glimpsed as a smudge.

  4. 6

    Class 6: Bright suburban sky

    Faintest star · magnitude 5.1 to 5.5

    The sky glows grey-white up to about 35° above the horizon. The Milky Way shows only straight overhead, and only just. All seven stars of the Little Dipper are back.

  5. 5

    Class 5: Suburban sky

    Faintest star · magnitude 5.6 to 6.0

    Light domes sit over towns in most directions, and clouds look brighter than the sky behind them. The Milky Way is weak overhead and washed out toward the horizon.

  6. 4

    Class 4: Rural/suburban transition

    Faintest star · magnitude 6.1 to 6.5

    The glow of distant towns sits low on the horizon. Overhead, the Milky Way is still impressive, though its fine detail is gone. Clouds are lit only toward the towns.

  7. 3

    Class 3: Rural sky

    Faintest star · magnitude 6.6 to 7.0

    A little glow along the horizon, and nothing above it. The Milky Way looks complex, split by dark lanes of dust. The Triangulum Galaxy appears if you look slightly beside it.

  8. 2

    Class 2: Typical truly dark site

    Faintest star · magnitude 7.1 to 7.5

    Airglow shows faintly near the horizon. The Milky Way is highly structured, the Triangulum Galaxy is easy to see, and clouds appear only as black holes in the stars.

  9. 1

    Class 1: Excellent dark-sky site

    Faintest star · magnitude 7.6 to 8.0

    Zodiacal light, gegenschein and airglow are all visible. The Triangulum Galaxy is plain to the naked eye. The brightest parts of the Milky Way cast faint shadows, and Venus or Jupiter can spoil your night vision.

This is the same sky.

Nothing up there changed between class 9 and class 1. Only the light underneath it.

Fewer stars every year. Three published findings.

  1. 1 in 3

    More than a third of humanity can no longer see the Milky Way from home. In Europe the figure is 60 percent.2

  2. 9.6%

    The night sky brightened by about 9.6 percent a year between 2011 and 2022, measured by more than 50,000 citizen observations of which stars people could still see.3

  3. Snow

    Snow throws street light back up into the sky, and low cloud throws it back down. Together they can make a suburban night brighter than a full moon.4 From late November to mid-January the sun does not rise at this latitude, and the lamps burn through the day.

The ordinance

Three words for the council.

We are not asking for a dark city. We are asking that every street light Brekkøy installs from now on meets three conditions, each written so it can go straight into the ordinance.

  1. 01

    Shielded

    Fully shielded fittings: no light emitted above the horizontal.

    A globe lamp sends its light every way at once: onto the street, into bedroom windows, and straight up, where it lights nothing but the sky. A hood stops it at the line, and the street gets the light it was paying for.

  2. 02

    Warm

    A colour temperature of 3000 K or lower.

    Blue-rich white light scatters in the atmosphere far more than warm light does, so it builds more skyglow from the same lamp. It is also the part of the spectrum that most suppresses melatonin. The American Medical Association recommended 3000 K or lower for street lighting in 2016.5

  3. 03

    Dimmable

    Fittings that can be dimmed, and are, in the hours when the streets are empty.

    Bright when the street is busy, low when it is not. The night bus and the snowplough still see the road. “Controlled” is one of the five principles for responsible outdoor lighting from DarkSky International and the Illuminating Engineering Society: light when it is needed, dimmed when possible.6

The street above is an illustration. How much darker a town’s sky gets depends on its fittings, its snow and its weather, and the letter asks the council to measure it.

Three fair questions.

Won’t the streets get darker?
The ordinance is about where the light goes, not how little of it there is. A shielded fitting aims its light down at the street it is meant to light, instead of into windows, eyes and sky.
Why 3000 K, and not lower?
3000 K is the ceiling the American Medical Association recommended and DarkSky International uses. The letter sets it as a maximum, so the council can choose warmer where it suits a street.
What will it cost?
That is the council’s figure to produce, and the letter asks for it. The ordinance sets a standard for every fitting from here on, so that each replacement is the right one.

Add your name and it goes on the letter. No petition counter, no follow-up you didn’t ask for.

To the members of Brekkøy municipal council,

We ask the council to adopt a dark-sky lighting ordinance, so that every street light installed or replaced in Brekkøy from its adoption is:

  1. Shielded, emitting no light above the horizontal;
  2. Warm, at a colour temperature of 3000 K or lower;
  3. Dimmable, and dimmed in the hours when the streets are empty.

We also ask the council to measure the night sky over the town before and after, and to publish the cost.

How the sky is drawn

About 85 catalogue stars at their real positions and magnitudes, fainter stars modelled at the real rate of roughly three times more per magnitude, and the Milky Way along the galactic plane. A star is drawn only if it is brighter than the faintest star for that Bortle class.
